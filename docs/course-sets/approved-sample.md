# Course-Set Comment Wording: Sample

Three skill groups split into course sets, each with its own phrase, next steps and six Browse sentences. Read with she/her filled in. Next steps name a skill or work habit to practise, not a content task.

## ADST: Competing Factors (adst-competing-factors)

**Old shared wording (every course):** she can weigh social, ethical and sustainability concerns in a design.

Old next steps: Emerging: name one social or ethical concern in a design. Developing: explain how a sustainability concern changes a design. Proficient: analyze how competing concerns shape a solution. (No Extending step.)

### ADST 9 and the Grade 10 Design and Technology Courses

**Courses:** Grades 8–9: Applied Design, Skills and Technologies 9. Grades 10–12: Computer Studies 10, Drafting 10, Electronics and Robotics 10, Entrepreneurship and Marketing 10, Media Design 10, Metalwork 10, Power Technology 10, Technology Explorations 10, Woodwork 10.

**Ministry wording:** Analyze and prioritize competing factors (ADST 9 adds social, ethical and sustainability) to meet community needs for preferred futures.

**Replaces:** she can weigh social, ethical and sustainability concerns in a design.

#### Grades 8–9

**Phrase:** she can decide which concerns matter most when a design is meant to help the community.

**Next steps** (her next step is to …)

- Emerging: practise naming the concerns a design raises for the community
- Developing: practise giving a reason for which concern matters most in a design
- Proficient: practise weighing two concerns against each other before making a design choice
- Extending: practise explaining how a design choice helps the community in the future

**Browse sentences**

- Strength: She can say how a design might help or harm people in her community.
- Strength: When two concerns do not fit together, she chooses one and explains why it matters more.
- Next (Developing): Her next step is to practise asking who a design leaves out each time she plans one.
- Next (Proficient): Next, she can practise asking how a design would change if another concern came first.
- Next (Extending): To go further, she can practise thinking about how a design will affect the community years from now.
- Home: At home, ask her what she would change about your neighbourhood, and who the change would help.

#### Grades 10–12

**Phrase:** she can prioritize competing concerns when a design meets a community need.

**Next steps** (her next step is to …)

- Emerging: practise naming the concerns that conflict in a community design
- Developing: practise explaining the order of priorities behind each design choice
- Proficient: practise weighing the trade-offs between priorities with evidence
- Extending: practise testing a set of priorities against the community's needs and hopes

**Browse sentences**

- Strength: She can explain which group in the community each concern speaks for.
- Strength: She ranks the concerns in a design and gives a reason for the order she chooses.
- Next (Developing): Her next step is to practise stating the cost of each priority in plain terms.
- Next (Proficient): Next, she can make a habit of writing her priorities down before she commits to a design.
- Next (Extending): To go further, she can practise judging how her priorities shape the future the community wants.
- Home: At home, ask her which should count more when buying something, its price or its effect on the planet.

### Family Studies

**Courses:** Family and Society 10, Interpersonal and Family Relationships 11, Child Development and Caregiving 12, Housing and Living Environments 12.

**Ministry wording:** Analyze (potential) competing factors to meet individual, family and community needs for preferred futures.

**Replaces:** she can weigh social, ethical and sustainability concerns in a design.

#### Wording

**Phrase:** she can explain how the needs of a person, a family and a community can compete.

**Next steps** (her next step is to …)

- Emerging: practise naming each person's needs in a family situation
- Developing: practise explaining how meeting one need affects the others
- Proficient: practise weighing individual, family and community needs before choosing a response
- Extending: practise judging which response serves a family's future, and explaining why

**Browse sentences**

- Strength: She can explain how a choice that suits one family member can strain the rest of the family.
- Strength: She connects a family's choices to the needs of the wider community.
- Next (Developing): A next step for her is to practise asking who gains and who loses in a family decision.
- Next (Proficient): Next, she can practise giving reasons for which need should guide a decision.
- Next (Extending): To go further, she can practise thinking ahead to how a choice made today shapes a family's future.
- Home: At home, ask her how your family decides when two people need different things.

### Food Studies 10

**Replaces:** she can weigh social, ethical and sustainability concerns in a design.

**Next steps** (her next step is to …)

- Proficient: practise weighing two competing concerns before deciding how to prepare food

**Browse sentences**

- Home: When you shop or cook together, ask her why she would choose one food over another.

**Browse sentences**

- Next (Extending): To go further, she can practise supporting each judgment about a textile item with facts about its making.
- Home: At home, ask her where one piece of her clothing was made and what that might mean.

**Next steps** (her next step is to …)

- Home: At home, ask her which items in your closet were made in Canada and which were made abroad.

### Social Studies 8 and Explorations in Social Studies 11

**Courses:** Grades 8–9: Social Studies 8. Grades 10–12: Explorations in Social Studies 11.

**Ministry wording:** Assess the significance of people, places, events or developments (Explorations 11 adds phenomena and ideas). No comparison of perspectives.

**Replaces:** she can explain why a person, place or event mattered.

#### Grades 8–9

**Phrase:** she can give reasons why a person, place or event was important at the time.

**Next steps** (her next step is to …)

- Emerging: practise saying who or what changed because of an event
- Developing: practise judging importance by how many people something affected
- Proficient: practise judging importance by how widely and how long an event was felt
- Extending: practise giving reasons and evidence when ranking how important things were

**Browse sentences**

- Strength: She can describe how an event changed daily life for the people it touched.
- Strength: She gives reasons, in her own words, why a person or development was important.
- Next (Developing): Her next step is to practise asking how long the effects of an event lasted.
- Next (Proficient): Next, she can practise ranking two events by importance and defending the order.
- Next (Extending): To go further, she can practise explaining how something from the past affects life today.
- Home: At home, ask her about an event from your family's past and why it was important.

#### Grades 10–12

**Phrase:** she can assess the significance of people, events, ideas and developments using clear criteria.

**Next steps** (her next step is to …)

- Emerging: practise giving one reason a person, idea or event was significant
- Developing: practise using criteria, such as reach and lasting impact, to judge significance
- Proficient: practise explaining how the significance of an idea can change over time
- Extending: practise weighing the significance of two developments and defending a judgment

**Browse sentences**

- Strength: She can explain why an idea or development was significant and who it affected.
- Strength: She uses criteria, such as how many people were affected, to judge an event's significance.
- Next (Developing): Her next step is to practise testing a claim of significance against what happened afterward.
- Next (Proficient): Next, she can practise noticing when a development that seemed small became significant later.
- Next (Extending): To go further, she can practise answering an opposing view when she argues that an idea mattered most.
- Home: At home, ask her which invention or idea from your lifetime has mattered most, and why.

## Judgement Calls

- English: in the data, `ela-information-use` holds only six Grade 12 courses: Composition, Creative Writing, English Studies, Literary Studies, New Media and Spoken Language. English 10–11 and English First Peoples are in other groups, so they get no set here.
- Some courses share one Ministry wording but are split by subject. Tourism 11 has the Media Design wording, and the business courses have the same global-needs wording as Computer Programming and Graphic Production.
- Two sets mix subjects because their wording is identical and the sentences only say "a design": Technology, Trades and Drafting (22 courses), and ADST 9 with the Grade 10 courses (Woodwork alongside Entrepreneurship and Marketing 10).
- Food Studies 10 is split from Food Studies 11–12, and Textiles 10–11 from Textiles 12. In each case the older course's wording names a different scope.
- Contemporary Indigenous Studies 12 is its own set. The bank-build README already moves it to its own group.
- Every next step starts with "practise" or "make a habit of", following the Nov 4 rule that next steps name a skill or habit. In Generate this opening will repeat across subjects.
